# A Lean Artificial Intelligence Major for a Liberal Arts College

## Furman University
Contributed by Chris Alvin (chris.alvin@furman.edu)

## Institutional and departmental context
- Location: Greenville, South Carolina, USA
- Undergraduate student body size: 2,500 students
- Degree(s) offered: B.A. and B.S. in Computer Science; B.A. and B.S. in Information Technology; B.S. in Artificial Intelligence; B.S. in Data Science
- Department/major name: Department of Computer Science
- Number of contributing faculty: 6 FTE
- Number of majors annually: 30 to 35 across the department; the AI major launched in Fall 2026 and has no declared majors yet
- Does the department offer any graduate programs? No
- Other context: The AI major sits beside an interdisciplinary Digital Ethics minor, which Alvin co-founded. A campus AI Strategic Task Force supports faculty AI use across disciplines.

## Description of Curricular Innovation

**Motivation.** AI is no longer a subfield that computing students may choose to study. It now shapes nearly every area of computing, and generative AI tools have become part of daily work in software development, research, and the professions our graduates enter. Students now want to understand how these systems work, where they fail, and how to use them responsibly. A liberal arts college is well positioned to combine that technical depth with judgment, provided the program fits a small department.

Most new AI degrees are housed in engineering schools or large research universities. Benchmarking against those programs pointed toward a major of 17 or more courses, many new course preparations, and several new faculty lines. None of those were realistic for a six-person department. We instead designed a 12-course (48-credit) B.S. in Artificial Intelligence that shares most of its foundation with our Computer Science and Data Science majors. Only three courses were new at launch, all of them AI electives: CSC-373 Natural Language Processing, CSC-374 Explainable AI, and CSC-375 Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence. The compact size leaves room for the general education curriculum, a second major, study away, and undergraduate research.

**A 200-level bridge course.** We moved our upper-level Artificial Intelligence course (formerly CSC-343) to the 200 level as CSC-243, Introduction to Artificial Intelligence. The course surveys search, machine learning, language, and responsible AI. It serves as the single prerequisite gateway to every 300-level AI elective. Students see the breadth of the field before they specialize, and the earlier entry point opens the major to students who discover AI through another discipline.

**Responsible AI as a thread, not a module.** Every major completes CSC-271, Digital Dilemmas, a course on data, technology, and what it means to be human. The course also anchors the interdisciplinary Digital Ethics minor. AI majors therefore share a classroom with students from the humanities and social sciences. Responsible AI does not end there. Each 300-level elective defines and practices responsible AI within its own domain. Students in Machine Learning with Big Data examine consent, provenance, and sampling bias in large datasets, along with the energy cost of training at scale. Students in Natural Language Processing audit language models for bias. Students in Explainable AI make a model's behavior accountable to the people it affects. Students in Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence design systems that keep people in meaningful control.

**Mathematics as proof.** Students may take MTH-343, Mathematical Theory of Artificial Intelligence, as an AI elective. It is a proof-based mathematics course. It traces the formal mathematical development of neural networks from the perceptron through backpropagation. The course builds on linear algebra (MTH-160) and the proof skills students develop in Discrete Structures (CSC-261). Students therefore understand why learning algorithms work, not only how to call them.

**Research as a capstone path.** Students may satisfy the capstone through faculty-mentored research (CSC-502) as well as the capstone seminar. Faculty research in drug discovery, game AI, and intelligent tutoring offers AI majors projects suited to conference presentation.

**Potential for adoption.** A small department can offer a credible AI major with only three new courses. The key moves are a 200-level bridge course, responsible AI carried through every elective, and a proof-based elective offered with Mathematics.

## Challenges/Limitations

**Challenges we face.**

- The major is new and has no declared majors yet; recruiting students and advising them alongside the CS and Data Science majors is an early task.
- Six faculty must staff five AI electives alongside existing CS offerings.
- The field changes quickly, which requires frequent revision of the bridge course and electives.
- Moving the AI course to the 200 level requires care to keep the 300-level electives rigorous. Discussions are ongoing about whether the single prerequisite creates a bottleneck for the 300-level electives.

**Challenges for adopters.**

- Hiring faculty with AI expertise is difficult at small colleges.
- Compute resources for large-scale machine learning coursework may be limited.
- A proof-based mathematics course depends on a willing partner department.
